01

Title & Abstract

Content review teams cannot manually check every page on a site — so which page should they look at first?

This project scores and ranks content pages by refresh opportunity, using only signals that were observable before any decision was made. A hand-written baseline rule built on staleness and visibility was tested against the data and found to point the wrong way; a signal grounded in CTR-versus-position, by contrast, held up cleanly and was used to build a working ranked queue of 13,698 candidate pages. A Random Forest classifier, trained and evaluated on an entirely held-out set of clients, then improved on that baseline — Precision@50 rose from 0.56 to 0.74 on the same split. The strongest single lever the model found was not staleness, the intuition behind the original rule, but a combination of impressions, position, and word count. This page documents the project honestly at its current stage: framing, data contract, baseline, and a first working model — with feature expansion and a future-window label planned next.

02

Introduction

FlyRank's content teams manage far more pages than they have time to review. A page can lose search visibility for many reasons — it went stale, a competitor overtook it, demand shifted, or the title no longer matches what people search for — but a reviewer's attention is the scarce resource in every case. The decision this project supports is simple to state and hard to do well: of all the pages on a site, which ones are worth a reviewer's next hour?

The cost of getting this wrong is not catastrophic but it is real. Flag a fine page as urgent, and the team wastes limited review time on it while a genuinely declining page waits longer to be noticed. Miss a real decline, and visibility keeps eroding unnoticed. Because both failure modes cost attention rather than money or safety, the right frame for this project is decision support, not automation: a ranked list with reasons a human can inspect, not a system that acts on its own.

What was excluded, and why

No product-computed decision flags — health_score, priority_score, action_type — are present in this dataset by design, and none were reconstructed and fed back in as features. Doing so would let a model simply copy an existing rule rather than discover real signal, a trap this project deliberately avoids throughout. Raw queries, URLs, titles, and client identities are pseudonymized upstream and were never re-identified or displayed.

04

Methodology

Task framing

This is a scoring problem, not classification or clustering: the output is a continuous priority score per page, sorted, with the reviewer working down the list from the top. The proxy label used during development, is_declining_label = (trend_direction == "down"), is a current-window bucket rather than a genuinely future outcome — a known limitation carried forward from the starter pipeline, and one the modeling phase will address by defining a prior-window → future-window label instead.

Signal audit

Before encoding any rule, two candidate signals were tested directly against the data:

SignalVerdictFinding
Staleness → decline
days_since_last_update ≥ 180		OppositeStale pages declined less often (47.1%, n=174) than non-stale pages (54.2%, n=29,826)
CTR vs. position tierConfirmedMean CTR fell cleanly by tier: top_3 1.48 → page_1 0.65 → striking 0.32 → page_3_5 0.22 → deep 0.15
Word count → declineOpposite (of common belief)Decline rate rose with length: <1K words 20.7% → 3K+ words 59.5% (association, not causal)

The staleness signal — the intuition behind FlyRank's own refresh-flag logic — did not hold up on this data and was dropped rather than forced into the rule. This negative result is treated as a finding, not a failure: it is exactly the kind of check a fixed rule skips and a data-grounded one does not. The word-count result was equally counter-intuitive: longer pages declined more often here, most plausibly reflecting harder competition on deep, comprehensive pages rather than any quality effect of length itself.

Baseline rule

score = (tier_avg_ctr − page_ctr) × impressions_90d eligible = avg_position > 0 and impressions_90d ≥ 500 and ctr_gap > 0 reason_code = "ctr_below_position_norm" action = "review_ctr_and_metadata"

The rule flags pages that are visible, have a real ranking position, and under-perform their own position tier's average click-through rate — scaled by impression volume, so high-traffic underperformers surface first.

Validation design (planned)

Once a learned model is trained, evaluation will use client-holdout validation — whole clients withheld from training so the model is tested only on clients it has never seen — matching the approach used elsewhere in this track and avoiding the inflated results a random row-level split would produce.

Three features — impressions, position, and word count — account for roughly 70% of the model's decisions. Consistent with the Week-4 signal audit, days_since_last_update (staleness) is the least important feature the model uses, reinforcing that staleness alone is a weak signal on this data. The model also draws on word_count, a feature the hand-written baseline never used at all — evidence that a fixed rule was leaving real signal on the table.

Where the model likely struggles

Pages with very low impressions_90d carry less reliable scores, since the model has fewer comparable examples to learn from at low volume — the baseline rule sidestepped this by requiring impressions_90d ≥ 500 outright, while the model has no equivalent explicit safeguard. A useful next check is Precision@50 computed separately for high-volume and low-volume pages, to confirm the model's advantage holds at both ends rather than being driven entirely by high-traffic pages.

Top-flagged pages (baseline rule)

The ten highest-scoring pages under the baseline rule all sit in strong ranking positions (top_3 or page_1) with click-through rates far below their tier's average — several under 0.10 against tier averages near 0.65–1.48. Two of the top twenty warrant a second look before acting: one page shows a CTR of 0.01 at a top-3 position with over 500,000 impressions, and another shows an exact 0.00 CTR on nearly 209,000 impressions. Both are flagged for manual verification against raw click logs rather than immediate action, since numbers this extreme are as likely to reflect a tracking issue as a genuine content problem.

06

Validation Rigor

Two additional checks were run specifically to stress-test this project's own honesty, mirroring the same audit applied earlier to FlyRank's public research paper.

Split design: does the split itself inflate the result?

Split typePrecision@50
Naive random split (rows mixed across clients)0.98
Client-holdout split (honest — used everywhere else in this report)0.74

A plain random split on the same model and features produced Precision@50 = 0.98 — nearly perfect, and almost certainly misleading. Under that split, pages from the same client can sit in both train and test, letting the model partly memorize client-specific patterns rather than learn a signal that generalizes to a new client. The 24-point gap between 0.98 and 0.74 is treated as a warning: any single "great" number should prompt the question "what split produced this?" before it is trusted or published.

Leakage audit: does the honest feature set actually leak?

Feature setAUC
Clean feature set (7 features, no label-derived fields)0.630
Same set + trend_pct deliberately added1.000

Adding trend_pct — the exact field the label is computed from — pushed AUC from an honest 0.630 to a suspicious 1.000, confirming the leak-detection method works and that trend_pct was correctly excluded from every feature set used elsewhere in this project. The overlap check between the seven production features and the label-derived fields (trend_direction, trend_pct) returned an empty set.

Applying the same scrutiny to the source research paper

The same audit discipline was turned on FlyRank's own public research paper, The State of AI-Driven SEO (April 2026). Two of its findings were reviewed with a constructive methodology question rather than taken at face value:

  • Freshness Multiplier claim (52× impression lift): this figure comes from a cohort of only 802 pages with just 21 declining — small enough that the multiplier could be driven by a handful of outlier pages rather than a stable pattern, and it does not appear in the paper's own significance-testing table alongside its other six tested claims.
  • Growth Prediction model (90% same-brand vs. 75% new-brand accuracy): a 15-point accuracy drop between same-brand and new-brand evaluation is the same shape of problem this report found in its own model (the 0.98-vs-0.74 split gap above) — raising a fair question about whether the "same-brand, new pages" evaluation used a split strict enough to rule out near-duplicate pages leaking across train and test.
07

Limitations & Honest Framing

  • Proxy label, not a future outcome. is_declining_label is a current-window bucket. A stronger, leakage-checked future-window label (prior 90 days → next 30 days) is planned but not yet built.
  • Unbalanced panel. Only 9 of 70 warehouse clients have 12+ months of history; cross-client comparisons in a single month mix clients with very different amounts of context.
  • Thin GA4 coverage. Only 4.2% of March 2026 rows have GA4 data available, and this is not random — it reflects when each client's tracking began.
  • Single-month development. All warehouse-based work so far uses one mid-panel month; nothing has been checked yet against a second month for seasonality.
  • No causal claim. A high score means "worth reviewing first," not "guaranteed to recover if refreshed." Proving that would require a controlled experiment this data cannot provide.
  • No algorithm claim. Nothing here observes or infers anything about Google's actual ranking algorithm — only FlyRank's own measured search and engagement data.

Findings in this report are described as observed, measured, or directional — never as proof.

Each recommendation is scoped to what this data can actually support:

  1. 01 Review title and meta description on the highest-scoring pages first — strong position with weak CTR most often points to a mismatch between what the snippet promises and what the searcher expects.
  2. 02 Before editing, sanity-check any page with a near-zero CTR against raw click logs — extreme values are as likely to be a tracking artifact as a real content problem.
  3. 03 Treat this list as a starting point for review, not a work order — with a validated Precision@50 of 0.74, roughly 1 in 4 top-ranked pages will still be a false positive, so every recommendation needs a human check before action is taken.
